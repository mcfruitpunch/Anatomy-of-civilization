## New text-level finding: Mercure de France, March 1731, pp. 610–611

The Bureau du Mercure page for the recurring article “Arrêts, déclarations, ordonnances, etc.” includes an entry on printed p. 610 beginning “AUTRE du 23. Janvier,” followed by “AUTRE du même jour, concernant la rétrocession...” and continuing on p. 611. The latter is dated by context to the same 23 January 1731 act identified on the UMSL title page.

The OCR transcription reports that the King accepted the Company's retrocession of the Louisiana and Illinois concession, its property/seigneurie/jurisdiction and associated posts and military assets, as well as its exclusive trade privilege. It then says Louisiana commerce is free to the King's subjects and preserves the Company's rights against its debtors in the province.

The key OCR-transcribed phrase is:

> “Maintient, S.M. ladite Compagnie dans les droits qu'elle a contre ses débiteurs de ladite Province”

**Exact locator:** *Mercure de France*, March 1731, printed pp. 610–611; Bureau du Mercure OCR transcription, lines 1582–1610 of the recurring-article page:
https://bureaudumercure.org/mercure/term/recurring_article?title=18303

The portal explicitly says it is exploratory and only partially accessible, with provisional and incomplete content. I read the OCR text, not the original periodical page image. This is a contemporary periodical extract, not a diplomatic transcription of the complete royal decree.

## Legal reading: creditor claims are not Company liabilities

**Documented in the contemporary extract:** the Company was maintained in rights it held against its debtors in Louisiana. This is a Company-side creditor/receivable provision: the royal text, as reproduced, preserves the Company's ability to pursue claims against debtors in the province.

**Not established:** the extract names no debtor, identifies no specific account or amount, and proves no suit, enforcement, collection, receipt or payment. It does not say that the Crown assumed the Company's own debts. It must not be conflated with the separate **24 February 1731** Council of State decree catalogued as concerning payment of the Company's Louisiana debts (1731 Imprimerie royale and 1733 Saugrain–Prault printings). Those operative clauses remain unread.

The event is therefore updated only to record a reserved Company creditor right. The financial ledger remains unchanged: `paid`, `transfer` and `liable` stay null for both 23 January and 24 February records.

## Independent scholarly cross-check

Erwan (Yevan) Terrien, *Exiles and Fugitives: Labor, Mobility and Power in French Colonial Louisiana, 1699–1769* (PhD diss., University of Pittsburgh, 2020), pp. 144–145, especially notes 100–102, discusses the Company's stated reasons for retrocession, the free-trade wording, and its implications for the slave trade. Footnote 102 identifies the 23 January 1731 decree as BNF-M, F-21114, Gallica ARK:
https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/btv1b86025746

Terrien's interpretation that the decree's free-trade wording only implicitly included the slave trade is a scholarly interpretation, not a clause that should be silently inserted into the decree. His broader argument that African and Indigenous resistance contributed to the end of Company rule is likewise a historical interpretation grounded in a wider evidentiary base, not a statement made by the decree.

The Gallica ARK was opened on 11 November 2026 but returned HTTP 403 in this environment. It is a verified retrieval identifier, not an inspected image.

## Affected-community context

The French legal text describes imperial concession, property, jurisdiction and trade as matters settled between the Crown and the Company. It does not record Indigenous consent to French claims over Louisiana or the Illinois country. The Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma's community-authored *Iti Fabvssa* history documents Choctaw diplomatic agency and negotiation in the 1729 trade-post arrangements; that perspective must remain distinct from French administrative claims and cannot be used as proof of consent to this decree.

Terrien's history-from-below analysis also centers the resistance, mobility and agency of enslaved Africans, Indigenous people, convicts and other coerced workers. A corporate or royal legal clause cannot stand in for their voices or lived experience.
