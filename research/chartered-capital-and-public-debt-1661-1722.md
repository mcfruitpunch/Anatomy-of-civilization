# V0.6 — Public debt, chartered privileges, and the South Sea crisis (1661–1722)

**Project question:** When governments needed money, what commercial privileges did they offer, who supplied capital, what financial institutions emerged, and what records distinguish a debt or agreement from individual gains?

This memo is an entry point, not a comprehensive interpretation of Britain's early empire. Relevant records should be compared with scholarship from regions affected by imperial commerce and enslaved peoples' histories.

## Documented institutional history

- **1661:** The East India Company's charter from Charles II expanded its ability to govern settlements, raise armed forces, and coin money. A British Library archival history records an aggregate **£324,150 in loans to the King over 1660–1684**; that is a period total, **not** a single payment or proof that every privilege was purchased. Source: `src-bl-eic-1698` (IOR/A/1 administrative context).
- **1694:** Investors subscribed **£1.2 million** to a chartered Bank of England to finance government war borrowing. The government paid an **8% interest rate**, backed by tonnage and excise taxation. The Bank of England's own charter and shareholder lists survive. Source: `src-boe-founder-1694`, `src-boe-tax-security`, `src-boe-1694-archive`.
- **1698:** Chancellor Charles Montagu advanced a borrowing arrangement with a **£2 million subscription program** connected to East India trading access. A General Society and a new English Company Trading to the East Indies were chartered. The old East India Company held **£315,000 in stock of the rival** in its treasurer's name. This is documented equity, not a newly proved cash payment. Source: `src-bl-eic-1698`.
- **1702–1709:** Rival East India corporations reached a coalition, reconciled interests and united their stock and trade; the old company surrendered its charter in 1709. Source: `src-bl-eic-1698`, `src-bl-eic-union-1708`.
- **1711–1720:** The South Sea Company developed a business in exchanging public debts for company equity and had Atlantic trading privileges, including trade in enslaved Africans. The South Sea Company and Bank of England competed over state-debt plans. A boom and crash occurred in 1720; some political officials received improper benefits, but culpability must be established individually rather than inferred from membership. Sources: `src-boe-ssc-archive`, `src-boe-ssc-competition`, `src-uk-na-asiento`.
- **1722:** Bank records include a **23 October agreement** concerning South Sea stock purchases and a separate circa-1722 proposal describing **£4.2 million**. These archival catalogue summaries should **not** be treated as a cash disbursement statement. Sources: `src-boe-ssc-agreement-1722`, `src-boe-ssc-proposals-1722`.

## How to read the financial trace records

| Amount | Record kind | What the source supports | What is not yet proven |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| £1.2m (1694) | Bank-to-government loan | Original government loan documented by Bank records | Which individual subscriber ultimately earned which cash returns |
| £2m (1698) | Crown borrowing/subscription scheme | Scheme and associated charters described in India Office catalog | Itemized receipts, repaid amounts, subscriber-level ledger |
| £315k (1698) | Old Company's equity position in rival | British Library says old Company owned this amount of new Company stock | Cash transferred on a specific day in 1698 |
| £4.2m (c.1722) | Bank–South Sea proposed deal | Archived proposal for payment and separately archived agreement | That all proposed funds were paid in cash on a specified date |

The database models each statement under a different `flow_state`. A chart or money-flow arrow must **never** erase these distinctions.

## Unanswered questions for named individuals

1. Which individuals appear in **both** Bank 1694 subscriber lists (**M1/1**, **AC27/382**, **AC27/414**) and East India subscriptions (**IOR/A/1/53–54**) or the 1708 share register (**IOR/B/50**)? Exact identification and chronology must be verified; shared surnames are not sufficient.
2. What positions did those subscribers hold when interest and dividends accrued? Who owned stock before versus after key votes?
3. Which 1720 Company director or parliamentary official had a documented improper allocation, and what is the best preserved original inquiry evidence? A secondary biographical note is a lead, not the transaction.
4. How did the 1694 tax backing distribute debt-service obligations across the population?
5. What original South Sea Company trading licence and voyage records (**T 52/26**, National Archives) document the **human costs** of enslavement, and whose testimony survives?
6. What competing constitutional and economic explanations do contemporaries and historians provide for the Bank's, East India companies', and South Sea Company's successes and failures?

## Critical guardrails

These are **not a single secret ownership bloc**. The Bank competed against the South Sea Company; the two East India firms competed before uniting. Following capital and legal privileges is not proof of an all-controlling dynasty, or of guilt through ancestry, religious identity or mere club membership. Preserve the distinct decisions of shareholders, officials, merchant workers, people in colonized territories and enslaved people.

**Method:** source-led entity relationships, historical events, event-to-event dominoes, financial records classified by evidentiary meaning, and explicit alternative interpretations.
