# V1.4: Louisiana card-money issuance/recall cross-check (26 November 2026)

**Bounded milestone for Issue #24.** This work does not read the February 24, 1731 Company-debt decree and does not treat card money as a Company-liability settlement. It distinguishes the 1735 authorization from a later official retrospective distribution assertion, a separate 30 April 1744 order reported in scholarship, and a 1746 archival lead.

## Witnesses examined

1. **Ernest Zay**, *Histoire monétaire des colonies françaises d'après les documents officiels* (1892), searchable text extraction, printed pp.181–183: ordinance dated Versailles, 14 September 1735, arts. I–VI, nominal 200,000-livre ceiling, denomination/signature rules, four procès-verbaux per fabrication, acceptance. This is a later edition of legal wording; the original 1735 imprint/registration and production records were not examined; page images were not visually examined in this run.
2. **Zay (1892), printed pp.184–187:** declaration dated 27 April 1744 on card money and treasury notes in Canada/Louisiana. It retrospectively states the 1735 200,000-livre quantity and that cards had been distributed for royal expenses; describes store-sales and treasury-bill acceptance; orders recall, reimbursement in bills of exchange, counted/burned cards with signed procès-verbaux, and a two-month deadline after registration. The phrase “à la réduction de 150 pour 100” is preserved without interpretation. The original 1744 declaration and execution records were not examined. Both instruments are in the same 1892 reprint and are not independent eighteenth-century witnesses.
3. **Nancy Maria Miller Surrey**, *The Commerce of Louisiana During the French Régime, 1699–1763* (1916), printed pp.126–127: secondary account of the 1735 ordinance and delayed implementation, with references AN C, Sér. A, vol.22 fol.146; vol.23 (14 Sep 1735); Sér. C13 vol.34 fols.376–378. The folios were not examined.
4. **Surrey (1916), p.138:** separately reports an order dated 30 April 1744 for Louisiana cards on a five-to-two basis, intended to circulate two months after promulgation, but says implementation was deferred because the French West Indies situation changed and war continued. Footnote cites AN C, Sér. B, vol.78 fols.42–51. This order is distinct from the 27 April declaration; its original text and execution remain unexamined.
5. **Surrey (1916), p.138, footnote 4:** reports a 1746 200,000-livre allowance used in exchange for Spanish silver expended on fortifications, citing AN C13 vol.31 fol.139. Which issue/allowance this refers to and the underlying exchange remain unresolved.

## Evidence distinctions

| Stage | What is supported | What is not verified |
|---|---|---|
| 14 Sep 1735 | Reprint authorizes up to 200,000 livres nominal card money and sets fabrication controls | Exact quantity fabricated/issued, cards, procès-verbaux, receipts |
| 27 Apr 1744 | Later royal declaration says cards had been distributed; orders recall/redemption procedures | Exact amount distributed, actual returned cards, completed redemption/payment |
| 30 Apr 1744 | Surrey reports separate five-to-two issue order; says implementation deferred | Original order, issue/fabrication, actual circulation |
| 1746 | Surrey reports 200,000-livre allowance/exchange and identifies folio | Transaction, identity of allowance, connection to prior orders, receipt |

The 1735 200,000 livres is an authorization ceiling, not a payment. The 1744 retrospective declaration provides a **reported distribution claim** but not a verified amount. The 27 and 30 April 1744 instruments are kept separate. No financial-flow or Company-liability record was created.

## Affected-community perspective

The 1735 and 1744 royal texts state that Louisiana inhabitants wanted or used the currency, but these are mediated official statements. Surrey's account likewise relies on colonial administrative dispatches. They do not establish direct consultation or consent from Choctaw, Natchez, other sovereign Indigenous nations, or enslaved people. The currency/credit record must remain paired with community-authored histories and evidence of coercion and manumission rather than treating colonial payment systems as the entire social history.
